## What I built

Demand & Supply Planning Intelligence is a retail planning tool. It evaluates demand
forecast methods against their own history, suggests what to buy and where to move stock
inside real operating limits, and shows what happens to those decisions when assumptions
change.

Planning fails in the gap between the forecast and what is actually possible. A forecast
says how much a store will sell; it does not say whether the supplier can dispatch in time,
whether the distribution centre can receive it, whether the calendar allows it, or whether
the money is available in the week the payment falls due. The tool keeps those questions in
one place, so a suggested order is one that could actually be placed.

Data and results are synthetic and modeled throughout — a Riyadh retail network built for
this project. Nothing here represents a company's operations, and no figure is an observed
result. It builds on my experience in demand planning, purchasing and inventory management.

## Demand Review

Three weekday-aware forecast methods — seasonal naive, a four-week weekday mean and a
recency-weighted weekday mean — are scored against withheld history in time order, so a
method is chosen on how it performed before the day it is used, not on the data it was
fitted to.

The screen reports the selected method and why it was selected, its quantity error against
the seasonal-naive baseline, pooled signed bias separating over- from under-forecasting, and
how many daily observations were actually scored. Dated fallbacks and data-quality warnings
stay visible rather than being smoothed away.

The forecast runs 56 days. The first 28 are the visible review window; days 29–56 are shown
as provisional and labelled that way wherever they appear. Expected demand is a model
estimate, not a service guarantee.

## Plan Review

The plan answers three questions: what to buy, where stock goes, and which demand remains
uncovered.

Purchases carry their supplier, order date, dispatch date, distribution-centre receipt date
and value. Orders inside the next seven days are marked release candidates; later orders
stay planned. Transfers move stock between locations, with the donor's own forecast demand
and buffer protected before any units leave it. Remaining shortages are listed per SKU and
store with the reason they could not be covered, separated into visible and provisional-tail
unmet demand.

Funding is dated, not a single budget line. Each week has a limit on new purchase
commitments and a ceiling on cash due, and existing obligations count against that ceiling
before anything new does. The screen shows commitments, payments, headroom and movement-fee
allowance week by week.

Every plan is compared against taking no new actions, on the same demand and the same
limits, so the plan's coverage is readable against the alternative of doing nothing.

## Scenarios

A scenario changes the assumptions — a promotion, a supplier receipt delay, reduced supplier
availability, tighter weekly funding — and produces three outcomes side by side:

- **Original** — the starting plan under the original assumptions.
- **Same actions** — those exact actions carried into the changed world, which is how a
  plan fails in practice: nobody replans the moment conditions move.
- **Replanned** — new actions calculated under the changed assumptions.

Separating the three keeps two different effects apart: what the change itself cost, and
what replanning recovered. Where the original actions become infeasible under the change,
the plan is shown as invalid with the dated constraint that broke it, rather than reported
as a worse number. Comparisons are estimates, not guaranteed savings.

## How it works

Synthetic dataset → forecast evaluation → constrained plan → independent replay → scenario
comparison

Planning is a mixed-integer program solved with SciPy's HiGHS backend over the full 56-day
horizon. Purchases and transfers are integer cases and packs, and the objectives run in
sequence rather than as one weighted score: service shortfall by store priority first, then
stock buffers, then cost. Each stage's result is held fixed before the next one runs, so
cost reduction cannot quietly trade away coverage. The model respects opening stock,
supplier capacity and lead times, case and pack sizes, receiving-calendar and volume limits,
and the dated funding limits above.

The resulting plan is then replayed independently against the same constraints, day by day,
to confirm it is actually executable. A plan that passes is reported as `feasible_fallback`:
it satisfies every constraint and has been checked, but it makes no claim of global
optimality, and shortages stay visible rather than being absorbed. The tool suggests; it
does not place orders.

## Build approach

- **Calculation in code.** Forecast scoring, constraints, the solver and the replay check
  are defined in code. No language model is involved in producing a number, a suggestion or
  a comparison.
- **Independent verification.** The plan is validated by a separate replay rather than by
  trusting the solver's own answer, so an infeasible plan surfaces as a failure instead of a
  recommendation.
- **Public demo runs on sample data.** Two bundled synthetic datasets are available: a
  10-SKU sample and a 60-SKU full sample of 240 SKU-store series. Workbook upload, review,
  acceptance and export are built and work locally or in Docker, but they depend on private
  storage that is intentionally unavailable on the public host.
- **Development.** I used Claude and Codex for implementation and QA, while defining the
  planning rules, constraints and expected outputs explicitly and validating results against
  the replay check and an automated test suite.
