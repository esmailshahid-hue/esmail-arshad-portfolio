## Context

The Amazon Freight program moves middle-mile volume across a lane network where roughly 100,000 shipments a week are priced, tendered and covered. Pricing on that network is not a rate card applied uniformly. It is a set of rules that has to clear volume on thousands of lanes with different lengths, densities and carrier appetites.

## Problem

A price that is too low on a lane does not fail loudly. The load simply does not get covered at that rate, and the network pays more later through re-tendering, expedites and service recovery. A price that is too high covers the load and quietly overpays across every shipment on that lane for as long as the rule stands.

Both failures look like normal operations from inside a single shipment. They are only visible in aggregate, at lane level, against how carriers actually respond.

## What I did

1. **Analysed lane-level demand** to establish where volume was concentrated and where carrier appetite was thin, rather than treating the network as one pricing environment.
2. **Brought carrier behaviour into the pricing inputs** — mileage, lead time and dwell time — so that lanes carrying real cost for a carrier were priced as such, and lanes that were cheap to run were not overpaid.
3. **Adjusted pricing rules dynamically** against those inputs, so prices moved with lane conditions instead of holding static between rate-card cycles.
4. **Measured the result at network level**, where the effect of a pricing rule is visible, rather than at the individual shipment level, where it is noise.

## Result

**8% reduction in freight network cost.**

The mechanism is capacity alignment rather than rate compression. Prices that reflect what a lane actually costs a carrier to run improve conversion on lanes that were previously hard to cover, and reduce overpayment on lanes that were easy — and the network cost falls out of both at once.

## Related work

A separate workstream in the same role ran root-cause analysis across roughly 100,000 weekly shipments in Tableau and Excel, examining carrier performance, shipment and service exceptions and SLA adherence, and initiating corrective actions — sustaining a 98% on-time delivery rate.

The two workstreams share a network and a dataset. They are not presented as one initiative, and the service-performance work is not claimed as the cause of the pricing result.
